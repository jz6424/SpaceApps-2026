The pitch

Train Junior Astronauts to keep a moon base alive

Our entry for the Space Apps challenge “Build a Junior Astronaut Mission Trainer”. Kids aged 7 to 12 build a base at the real lunar south pole and run short training missions where every choice costs something: power for the night, regolith for oxygen or for shielding, comms with Earth or science. Real NASA solar events drive the danger, and a debrief after each mission names the real constraint they just felt. Failing never ends the game; the base dims and explains what went wrong. See Challenge fit for the proposed reframe.

    Official challenge · Build a Junior Astronaut Mission Trainer

    From a fun base builder to a mission trainer

    The full challenge text (Nov 6) keeps the idea we designed for: kids run a lunar or Martian outpost and balance life support, radiation shielding, power and food. It adds sharper rules. Players are ages 7 to 12, communication delay joins the list, no choice may be free, kids should track only two or three core resources, and the game should run on real NASA data (DONKI space weather, OSDR astronaut health, Artemis and Mars surface data), credited inside the game. The goal is that a kid walks away understanding one real constraint of deep space survival, not just having played a fun game.

    The reframe in one line

    Each mission is a training mission for a Junior Astronaut. Every decision costs something real, a real NASA solar event drives the danger, and a short debrief after each mission names the real constraint the kid just felt and the NASA data behind it.

    Missions, reframed as training

    1. First Night (Power and Life Support). Same as now, plus one small real solar flare that teaches the Radiation Dose meter and the shelter button
    2. Solar Storm (Shielding). Was Storm Watch, now second. A real DONKI event from the archive; comms give the warning only if the antenna is on and Earth is up. Regolith for oxygen or for shielding
    3. Ice and Greens (Food and Water). Ice Hunters plus the Greenhouse for every mode, with the supplies counter

    Each mission ends with a debrief card: one real constraint in a sentence, the NASA data behind it, and one thing to try next time.

    Real NASA data we will use

    • DONKI (Space Weather Database): real solar flares and particle events set storm timing and strength. the menu can show the latest real event from NASA's API; inside missions we replay dated archive events, labeled with their dates
    • LRO LOLA terrain, sunlight and Earth-visibility maps (already in our site data): the ground, the shadows and the comms windows
    • LRO CRaTER and OSDR: radiation dose on the Moon and astronaut health findings for the debrief cards (numbers to verify)
    • Life support planning values (NASA 2019) and ISS water recovery: the rates behind Life Support

    Play from inside the habitat

    Jerry's direction (Nov 7): the habitat is big enough for the whole crew, and play centers on life inside it. That fits a mission trainer well, because real crews spend almost all their time indoors and send robots outside.

    • The control room is the dashboard: kids walk to stations for power, life support, comms and the storm shelter, and see the three meters on wall screens
    • Robots work outside: diggers, solar and the antenna are placed from a window or map table, like real teleoperation
    • Going outside is a choice with a cost: a moonwalk uses air and adds radiation dose, so kids save it for science or repairs
    • Shelter is a real room: in a storm the crew moves to the shielded core of the habitat, and outdoor work stops
    • Crew of 2 to 4: each astronaut uses air, water and food, so bringing more crew means more science but bigger needs

    What that means for the build

    • A larger habitat interior with stations (the Blender habitat redesign covers the shell)
    • A bigger, smoother map, so building outside stays easy to read from indoors
    • Station UI that shows one decision at a time, for ages 7 to 12
    • The grid placement math ports from the Roblox prototype and runs from the map table
    • Kids walk a third-person astronaut through the habitat and outside (Rapier physics); see Play & website

    Jerry's direction · Nov 7

    Walk your base: balance inside, explore outside

    The game is a real 3D world. Kids walk their astronaut through the habitat and out onto the Moon. Inside is where they balance the base: power, life support, shielding and food. Outside is where the mission goals and the science are. Every trip out leaves the base running on the settings the kid chose before leaving, so good planning inside is what makes exploring outside possible.

    Inside the habitat: keep everyone alive

    Safe, warm, shielded. Every station is a decision with a cost.

    • Power panel (lab): split power between life support, comms, science and the greenhouse
    • Life support rack (lab): oxygen maker and water recycler speeds; feed it regolith
    • Comms console (lab): talk to Earth while Earth is up; get storm warnings; send science home for research points
    • Galley and grow rack: food, and how much power the plants get
    • Map table: send robots to dig, build solar panels or shielding outside
    • Storm shelter (sleep bay core): where the crew waits out a solar storm
    • Airlock: suit up; check suit air before going out

    Outside on the Moon: goals and science

    Every moonwalk uses suit air and adds radiation dose.

    • Mission goals: unload the supply crate from the lander, set up the antenna on the ridge, find ice
    • Science stops: take an ice sample in a shadowed crater, drop a radiation sensor, photograph Earth, earning research points
    • Repairs: dust off solar panels, fix a panel hit by a micrometeoroid
    • Risk: a solar storm while outside means hurrying back. The warning only comes if the comms are on and Earth is in the sky
    • Wrist display: shows the three base meters and suit air, but settings only change back inside

    One mission, inside and out

    1. Plan inside. Read the mission card, set the power split, start the oxygen maker, send robots to dig.
    2. Go out. Suit up at the airlock and walk to a goal or science stop. Bouncy moon gravity makes walking fun.
    3. Something changes. A storm warning, a dusty panel, the sun getting low. Choose: push on for the science, or head home.
    4. Come back and rebalance. Refill suit air, shelter if a storm hits, save battery for the night.
    5. Make it through the night, then the debrief: stars, research points, the real constraint and its NASA data.

    Target pacing for ages 7 to 12: about two moonwalks per mission, each 2 to 4 minutes, with the base asking for a decision about once a minute.

    Walking: how we build it

    • Third-person astronaut, so kids can see where they are. WASD or arrow keys and mouse drag on a laptop, an on-screen joystick on a tablet, E or a tap to use a station
    • Physics: Rapier (@dimforge/rapier3d-compat). Its character controller handles walls, the habitat steps and slopes; terrain is a heightfield collider made from the NASA heightmap
    • Moon gravity: 1.62 m/s², about one sixth of Earth's, so jumps are high and slow
    • Habitat walls: simple invisible boxes named COL_… in Blender, not the detailed mesh
    • Camera pulls in and the roof fades when inside, so rooms stay readable

    How a play session works

    Pick a mission, play, debrief, repeat

    1. Mission menu. A Moon Base hangar screen shows stars, research points, unlocks and the latest real solar event. The player picks a mission and a mode.
    2. Launch. The mission loads in the same page; no account, no server.
    3. Match. One compressed lunar day.
    4. Debrief. 0 to 3 stars and research points, the real constraint the kid just felt, the NASA data behind it, and one thing to try next time. An unfinished mission still pays a little.
    5. Back to the menu. Stars and points are saved in the browser (localStorage), with a save code to carry progress to another device.

    The lesson in one chain

    No sun means no power. No power means no new oxygen, and a cold Habitat makes the crew breathe suit air faster. Batteries are how a solar base gets through the night, and that is why NASA wants nuclear power on the Moon.

          The real plan behind the game

          NASA announced an official Moon Base program in 2026, built in three phases near the lunar south pole. Those phases make natural game chapters.

          PhaseYearsWhat gets builtGame chapter
          1. Explore and test2026–2029Up to 25 robot missions, rovers, hopper drones, relay satellites, night-survival testsDigger robots and the first lander
          2. Early habitation2029–2032Better solar, first nuclear power, first small habitatsFirst Night, Ice Hunters, Storm Watch
          3. Sustained presence2032 onLong-stay habitats, a fission power station, pressurized roversThe Long Dark (fission reactor), then Mars

          Artemis II flew four astronauts around the Moon on April 1–10, 2026. Artemis III is a 2027 docking test in Earth orbit, and Artemis IV is the first planned landing, in 2028.
